# Policy Gradient: Expectation Form and Per-Timestep Decomposition

---

## User

*(Image of the following gradient estimate, with the advantage term highlighted.)*

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_{i,t} \mid \mathbf{s}_{i,t})\, A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_{i,t}, \mathbf{a}_{i,t})
$$

Write the expectation version of this gradient estimate. Be clear about what the expectation is taken over.

## Assistant

### Expectation form

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta(\tau)}\!\left[\, \sum_{t=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) \right]
$$

**What the expectation is over:** whole trajectories $\tau = (\mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{s}_T, \mathbf{a}_T)$ generated by running the **current** policy $\pi_\theta$ in the environment:

$$
p_\theta(\tau) \;=\; p(\mathbf{s}_1) \prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, p(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)
$$

The randomness comes from three sources: the initial state distribution $p(\mathbf{s}_1)$, the policy's action sampling $\pi_\theta$, and the environment dynamics $p(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$. The dynamics are unknown, so we can't compute this expectation analytically and must sample instead.

### Equivalent per-timestep form

Linearity of expectation lets you push it inside the sum, so each term only depends on the marginal at time $t$:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}_t \sim p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t),\; \mathbf{a}_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid \mathbf{s}_t)}\!\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) \right]
$$

Here $p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t)$ is the state marginal at step $t$ induced by following $\pi_\theta$.

### How it connects to your estimate

The expression in your image is the Monte Carlo approximation of this: draw $N$ trajectories $\tau_i \sim p_\theta(\tau)$ i.i.d., and replace the expectation with the sample average $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N$. A few points worth keeping straight:

- **The samples must be on-policy.** They come from $p_\theta$ for the current $\theta$, which is why vanilla policy gradient has to collect fresh data after each update.
- **$A^{\pi}$ is the advantage of $\pi_\theta$ itself**, i.e. $A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) = Q^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) - V^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t)$. It is a fixed function of $(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$, not something averaged over again inside the bracket.
- **Unbiasedness:** if $A^{\pi}$ is exact, the sample average is an unbiased estimate of the gradient, and the $\approx$ becomes $=$ as $N \to \infty$. In practice $A^{\pi}$ is itself estimated (e.g. with a learned critic), which can introduce bias in exchange for lower variance.

## User

Explain the per-timestep form. Are we following a single trajectory here or multiple rollouts? What happened to the initial state? Write down the integral form of the expectation.

## Assistant

### What the per-timestep form says

The trajectory form asks: "average the whole sum $\sum_t g_t$ over full trajectories." The per-timestep form rewrites that as $T$ separate averages, one for each time index $t$:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}_t \sim p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t),\; \mathbf{a}_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid \mathbf{s}_t)}\!\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) \right]}_{\text{depends only on the distribution of } (\mathbf{s}_t,\, \mathbf{a}_t)}
$$

The key observation is that the $t$-th term $g_t = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$ is a function of only $(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$. So when you average it over full trajectories, everything else in the trajectory (the past that led to $\mathbf{s}_t$ and the future after $\mathbf{a}_t$) can be integrated out, leaving only the **marginal** distribution of the pair $(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$ at step $t$.

### Single trajectory or multiple rollouts?

Neither, strictly. An expectation is a property of a distribution, not of any particular set of samples. Each term asks: "if I ran the policy from scratch infinitely many times and looked only at what happens at step $t$, what is the average of $g_t$?" So conceptually it involves the whole population of rollouts, viewed one time slice at a time.

When you go to estimate it, the same $N$ rollouts serve every term: rollout $i$ contributes the sample $(\mathbf{s}_{i,t}, \mathbf{a}_{i,t})$ to the $t$-th expectation for every $t$. This is exactly your double sum $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \sum_t$. Terms at different $t$ from the same rollout are correlated, but that doesn't matter for unbiasedness, since expectation is linear regardless of dependence.

One thing to notice: $p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_1), p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_2), \dots$ are generally **different** distributions. States at step 5 look different from states at step 50. So this is $T$ distinct expectations, not one expectation repeated $T$ times.

### What happened to the initial state?

It didn't disappear; it got absorbed into $p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t)$. The state marginal at step $t$ is defined by starting from $p(\mathbf{s}_1)$ and pushing forward through the policy and dynamics:

$$
p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_1) = p(\mathbf{s}_1), \qquad
p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_{t+1}) = \int\!\!\int p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t)\, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, p(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)\, d\mathbf{a}_t\, d\mathbf{s}_t
$$

Unrolled, that is

$$
p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t) = \int \cdots \int p(\mathbf{s}_1) \prod_{k=1}^{t-1} \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_k \mid \mathbf{s}_k)\, p(\mathbf{s}_{k+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_k, \mathbf{a}_k)\; d\mathbf{s}_1\, d\mathbf{a}_1 \cdots d\mathbf{s}_{t-1}\, d\mathbf{a}_{t-1}
$$

So $p(\mathbf{s}_1)$ is still there, buried inside the marginal. Only for $t = 1$ does it appear directly, and in that case $p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_1) = p(\mathbf{s}_1)$ doesn't even depend on $\theta$.

### Integral form

**Trajectory form.** With $p_\theta(\tau) = p(\mathbf{s}_1) \prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t) \prod_{t=1}^{T-1} p(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$ and $d\tau = d\mathbf{s}_1\, d\mathbf{a}_1 \cdots d\mathbf{s}_T\, d\mathbf{a}_T$:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \int p_\theta(\tau) \sum_{t=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)\; d\tau
$$

**Reducing to per-timestep.** Pull the sum out and look at a single term $t$. Split the integration variables into past ($k < t$), present ($k = t$), and future ($k > t$):

1. **Future integrates to 1.** Starting from the last step and working backward, $\int \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_T \mid \mathbf{s}_T)\, d\mathbf{a}_T = 1$, then $\int p(\mathbf{s}_T \mid \mathbf{s}_{T-1}, \mathbf{a}_{T-1})\, d\mathbf{s}_T = 1$, and so on down to $\int p(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)\, d\mathbf{s}_{t+1} = 1$. These factors vanish because $g_t$ doesn't depend on any future variables.
2. **Past collapses to the marginal.** The remaining integral over $\mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{s}_{t-1}, \mathbf{a}_{t-1}$ is exactly the definition of $p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t)$ above.
3. **Present is what's left:** $p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t)\, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)$ times $g_t$.

The result:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \int\!\!\int p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t)\, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\; \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)\; d\mathbf{a}_t\, d\mathbf{s}_t
$$

which is the per-timestep expectation written out as an integral.

A side note: $A^{\pi}$ is itself defined via expectations over the future, but inside this integral it is just a fixed function of $(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$. That's why step 1 is valid: the future randomness has already been "baked into" $A^{\pi}$ and doesn't need to be integrated again.

Explain the last statement. What do you mean by step 1?

## Assistant

### Which "step 1"

In the previous reply, reducing the trajectory integral to the per-timestep form had three steps. Step 1 was **"Future integrates to 1"**: for the $t$-th term, all the factors in $p_\theta(\tau)$ that involve variables after $\mathbf{a}_t$ were integrated away, each giving 1.

Concretely, the future part of $p_\theta(\tau)$ is

$$
p(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)\, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_{t+1})\, p(\mathbf{s}_{t+2} \mid \mathbf{s}_{t+1}, \mathbf{a}_{t+1}) \cdots \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_T \mid \mathbf{s}_T)
$$

and integrating it over $\mathbf{s}_{t+1}, \mathbf{a}_{t+1}, \dots, \mathbf{s}_T, \mathbf{a}_T$ gives 1, since it's a product of conditional densities (peel them off from the end backward).

**But that only works if the integrand $g_t$ contains none of those future variables.** If $g_t$ depended on, say, $\mathbf{s}_{t+1}$, you couldn't pull it outside the $d\mathbf{s}_{t+1}$ integral, and the future factors would not simply become 1.

### Why this looks suspicious

The advantage is defined in terms of the future:

$$
A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) = Q^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) - V^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t), \qquad
Q^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[ \sum_{t'=t}^{T} r(\mathbf{s}_{t'}, \mathbf{a}_{t'}) \;\middle|\; \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t \right]
$$

So a natural objection is: "$A^\pi$ is all about what happens after step $t$. How can we throw the future away?"

### The resolution: $A^\pi$ is a function, not a random quantity

The expectation inside the definition of $Q^\pi$ has **already been computed**. Written as an integral,

$$
Q^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) = \int \underbrace{p(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)\, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_{t+1}) \cdots}_{\text{future density}} \sum_{t'=t}^{T} r(\mathbf{s}_{t'}, \mathbf{a}_{t'})\; d\mathbf{s}_{t+1}\, d\mathbf{a}_{t+1} \cdots d\mathbf{s}_T\, d\mathbf{a}_T
$$

The future variables are integration variables here. Once integrated, they're gone. What remains is a deterministic function whose only inputs are $(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$. If you handed someone $Q^\pi$ as a lookup table, they could evaluate $Q^\pi(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$ without knowing anything about $\mathbf{s}_{t+1}$ or later. Same for $V^\pi(\mathbf{s}_t)$, and hence $A^\pi$.

That's what "baked in" meant: the future has been averaged over once, inside the definition of $A^\pi$. In the outer gradient integral, $A^\pi(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$ is no different from any other function of $(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$, so step 1 goes through.

### Contrast: when the integrand does depend on the future

This becomes clearer with an estimator that is **not** a fixed function of $(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$. In REINFORCE you use the sampled reward-to-go from the actual trajectory:

$$
\hat{Q}_t = \sum_{t'=t}^{T} r(\mathbf{s}_{t'}, \mathbf{a}_{t'})
$$

This depends on $\mathbf{s}_{t+1}, \mathbf{a}_{t+1}, \dots$ from the trajectory itself. Now step 1 does **not** give 1. Instead, integrating the future factors against $\hat{Q}_t$ gives

$$
\int (\text{future density})\, \hat{Q}_t \; d(\text{future}) = Q^{\pi}(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)
$$

by the very definition of $Q^\pi$ above. So the future integral doesn't vanish; it *produces* $Q^\pi$. The end result is the same per-timestep expression, just reached a different way:

| Integrand uses | Future integral gives | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $A^\pi(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$ (exact function) | $1 \times A^\pi(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$ | no future variables in it |
| $\hat{Q}_t$ (sampled return) | $Q^\pi(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$ | averaging the sample over the future recovers its mean |

This is also why the two choices give the same gradient in expectation but differ in variance: $A^\pi$ has done the future averaging analytically, while $\hat{Q}_t$ relies on a single sampled future per rollout and leaves the averaging to the outer $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i$.

One condition makes this all consistent: the future density inside $Q^\pi$ uses the same $\pi_\theta$ as the future factors in $p_\theta(\tau)$. That's why the advantage has to be that of the current policy.

Should there be an $\mathbf{s}_{T+1}$ state in this expression?

## Assistant

No. There's no $\mathbf{s}_{T+1}$ here, and no $\mathbf{s}_{t+1}$ either. The highest-index state in this expression is $\mathbf{s}_t$ itself.

**Tracking the indices.** The product runs over $k = 1, \dots, t-1$. The last factor, at $k = t-1$, is

$$
\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{s}_{t-1})\, p(\mathbf{s}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_{t-1}, \mathbf{a}_{t-1})
$$

so the transition at $k = t-1$ is what produces $\mathbf{s}_t$. The integrals cover $\mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{s}_{t-1}, \mathbf{a}_{t-1}$, which is every variable in the integrand except $\mathbf{s}_t$. That leftover free variable is exactly what makes the result a density over $\mathbf{s}_t$.

Written out for $t = 3$:

$$
p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_3) = \int\!\!\int\!\!\int\!\!\int p(\mathbf{s}_1)\, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_1 \mid \mathbf{s}_1)\, p(\mathbf{s}_2 \mid \mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{a}_1)\, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_2 \mid \mathbf{s}_2)\, p(\mathbf{s}_3 \mid \mathbf{s}_2, \mathbf{a}_2)\; d\mathbf{s}_1\, d\mathbf{a}_1\, d\mathbf{s}_2\, d\mathbf{a}_2
$$

Nothing after $\mathbf{s}_3$ appears, because the marginal of $\mathbf{s}_t$ only depends on how you got there, not on what happens next. For $t = 1$ the product is empty and there are no integrals, leaving $p_\theta(\mathbf{s}_1) = p(\mathbf{s}_1)$.

**Where $\mathbf{s}_{T+1}$ might have come from.** In my first reply I wrote the trajectory density with the transition product running to $T$:

$$
p_\theta(\tau) = p(\mathbf{s}_1) \prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, p(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)
$$

The last factor there is $p(\mathbf{s}_{T+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_T, \mathbf{a}_T)$, which does introduce $\mathbf{s}_{T+1}$. In the second reply I ran the transitions only to $T-1$, so the trajectory ends at $\mathbf{a}_T$. Both conventions give the same gradient: nothing in the objective or the integrand depends on $\mathbf{s}_{T+1}$, so integrating over it contributes a factor of $\int p(\mathbf{s}_{T+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_T, \mathbf{a}_T)\, d\mathbf{s}_{T+1} = 1$. Ending at $\mathbf{a}_T$ is just the tidier choice.
